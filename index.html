Guides

Short, practical explainers behind the numbers — each with the same worked example the calculator uses.

How much concrete do I need? (formula + worked example)

Concrete is sold by volume, so every slab question is really a volume question. Multiply the length by the width to get the area, then multiply by the thickness — remembering to convert thickness from inches into feet first by dividing by 12.

10 ft × 10 ft × (4 in ÷ 12) = 33.33 cu ft → 33.33 ÷ 27 = 1.23 cubic yards

Worked example: a 10 × 10 ft patio slab, 4 inches thick, needs 33.33 cubic feet of concrete — 1.23 cubic yards. Ordered as ready-mix, you'd ask for 1.23 yd³ (most suppliers round delivery to the nearest quarter or half yard, and a small extra allowance covers spillage). Mixing it yourself from bags: an 80-lb bag yields about 0.6 cu ft, so 33.33 ÷ 0.6 = 55.6, rounded up to 56 bags. With 60-lb bags (0.45 cu ft each) it's 33.33 ÷ 0.45 = 74.1, rounded up to 75 bags.

Two habits save projects: always round bag counts up (you can't buy 0.6 of a bag, and running short mid-pour leaves a weak cold joint), and add roughly 10% volume when the ground underneath is uneven, because dips quietly eat concrete.

How much paint for a room?

Paint the walls, not the room's floor area. The wall area of a rectangular room is the perimeter times the ceiling height: 2 × (length + width) × height. Then subtract what you won't paint — a standard door is about 21 sq ft and an average window about 15 sq ft.

2 × (12 + 10) × 8 = 352 sq ft − 21 (door) − 30 (2 windows) = 301 sq ft per coat

Worked example: a 12 × 10 ft room with 8 ft ceilings, one door and two windows has 301 sq ft of paintable wall. Two coats means covering 602 sq ft in total. At a typical coverage of 350 sq ft per gallon, that's 602 ÷ 350 = 1.72 gallons — so buy 2 gallons.

Why coats and coverage matter: coats multiply the area, they don't add a fixed amount — two coats of a dark colour over a light wall (or the reverse) genuinely need twice the paint of one coat. And coverage varies: the 350 sq ft figure assumes smooth, primed walls. Bare drywall, rough plaster and deep textures all drink more paint, so check the coverage printed on your tin and use that number in the calculator instead.

How to measure square footage (and why waste % matters)

For a rectangle, square footage is simply length × width, measured in feet. A 12 ft × 10 ft bedroom floor is 120 sq ft (11.15 sq m). Measure wall-to-wall at floor level, and for L-shaped rooms, split the floor into rectangles, work out each one, and add them. Circles use π × radius²; triangles use base × height ÷ 2.

12 ft × 10 ft = 120 sq ft · 120 × 0.0929 = 11.15 sq m

Why waste % matters: almost nothing is installed without cutting. Tiles get trimmed at edges and break; planks get cut at the end of every row. Buying exactly the measured area guarantees you run short. The standard fix is a waste allowance: 10% for straight layouts — so 100 sq ft of tiling needs 110 tiles' worth of coverage, and 200 sq ft of flooring at 20 sq ft per box needs 220 sq ft of coverage, which is exactly 11 boxes. Diagonal or patterned layouts waste more; use 15% or higher. The leftover is not wasted money — it's your repair stock when a tile cracks in three years and that design is discontinued.

Frequently asked questions

Quick answers to the most common project questions.

How much concrete do I need for a 10×10 slab?

A 10 ft × 10 ft slab at 4 inches thick needs 33.33 cubic feet of concrete, which is 1.23 cubic yards. If you are mixing bags yourself, that is 56 bags of 80-lb mix or 75 bags of 60-lb mix. For ready-mix delivery, order 1.23 cubic yards plus a small extra allowance for spillage and an uneven base.

How many gallons of paint do I need for a 12×10 room?

For a 12 ft × 10 ft room with 8 ft ceilings, one door and two windows, the paintable wall area is 301 sq ft. At two coats and 350 sq ft of coverage per gallon, you need 1.72 gallons, so buy 2 gallons. One coat would need about 0.86 gallons, so buy 1 gallon.

How much extra should I buy for waste?

For tile and flooring, 10% extra is the standard starting point for straight layouts in rectangular rooms. Use 15% or more for diagonal layouts, patterned tiles, or rooms with many corners and cuts. The waste allowance covers cuts, breakages and mistakes, and leaves you a few spares for future repairs.
